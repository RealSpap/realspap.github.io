We read 55 incidents from our public postmortems against the six requirements the oracle publishes for every contract. Most are bugs the score cannot see. The Drift case, start to end

  1. The setup. Drift's program admin was a Squads multisig of five signers where two approvals were enough, with no timelock between an approval and its effect.
  2. The preparation. Two signers were tricked, weeks earlier, into pre-signing transactions that use a durable nonce, which stay valid until used.
  3. The day. On 1 April 2026 the attackers broadcast them: the program admin was overwritten in one go, a fake token was listed as collateral and the withdrawal guards were raised. About $295.7M left (press: $285M).
  4. What the published requirements say about that setup. "At least a 3-of-5 multisig": not met, because two of five were enough. "A confirmed delay before changes apply": not met, because there was none. A reader who looked at the setup, not at the attack, had two reasons for concern the day before, and a month before.
  5. What they do not say. They do not see a signature that was collected off-chain and not yet used: a pre-signed transaction is invisible on-chain until it is broadcast, so the watch could not have raised an alert about the attack itself. The requirements flag the setup that made it possible, not the attack.

What this does not show

  • Not a score at the time. No historical on-chain state was re-read. The setup is the one the write-up gives; "flagged" means the published requirements would have been unmet on that setup, not that the oracle showed a particular number.
  • Small numbers. 7 cases are cases, not statistics.
  • Classification. 55 write-ups from RealSpap/onchain-postmortems read by automated readers, the 7 in-scope ones re-read by hand against the text; pre-incident configuration taken from the write-up only (stated, inferred or not stated); no historical on-chain state was re-read.
  • A hit is not protection. Two incidents passed the requirements and still lost money: a score about authority does not replace the security of whoever holds the keys.
